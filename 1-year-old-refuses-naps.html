1-Year-Old Refusing Naps: Understanding and Managing the Change

Around the age of one, many children suddenly start to resist their naps, which can be a source of stress for parents. This is often not a sign that they are ready to give up sleep entirely, but rather a developmental phase or a signal that their sleep schedule needs adjusting.

Is It a Nap Strike or a Schedule Change?

A child who refuses to nap may be going through a temporary &quot;nap strike&quot; or a true transition to a new sleep pattern. Understanding the difference is key to responding effectively.

The most common reason a 1-year-old begins refusing naps is that they are ready to drop from two naps to one . This transition usually happens between 12 and 18 months of age . A sign that it&#x27;s time for this change is if your child consistently fights their morning nap, takes a very long time to fall asleep for a nap, or the nap causes them to stay up too late at night.

How Much Sleep Does a 1-Year-Old Need?

To determine what is best for your child, it helps to know the general sleep guidelines. According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), children aged 1 to 2 years need 11 to 14 hours of sleep per day, including naps . This means that if your child gets 10hours of sleep at night, they will likely need about 1hours of daytime sleep, often in the form of a single afternoon nap.

How to Manage Nap Resistance

If your child is fighting naps, there are several strategies you can try:

Look for Sleep Cues: Put your child down for their nap as soon as they show signs of being tired, such as rubbing their eyes, yawning, or becoming fussy. Missing this window can lead to overtiredness, making it harder for them to fall asleep.

Adjust the Schedule: If your child is on a two-nap schedule, you might need to adjust the timing. As children get older, they need longer periods of wakefulness between naps. Try extending the time between the morning nap and the afternoon nap by 15minutes.

Consider the Transition: If your child is consistently fighting one of their naps, it might be time to drop it. To make the transition to one nap, you can gradually move the morning nap later and longer until it replaces the afternoon nap.

Quiet Time Instead of Naps: On days when your child absolutely refuses to sleep, a quiet time with calm activities, like reading books in their crib, can still provide a period of rest and relaxation.

When to Talk to Your Doctor

If you are worried about your child&#x27;s sleep patterns, if you are worried about possible delays, or if you have concerns about their overall development, talk to your child&#x27;s health care provider (Source: NIH | Toddler Development). They can help rule out any underlying issues and offer personalized advice.
