Diaper Rash: Emergency Signs Every Parent Should Know

Understanding Diaper Rash

A rash is an area of irritated or swollen skin. Many rashes are itchy, red, painful, and irritated. Some rashes can also lead to blisters or patches of raw skin (Source: NIH | Rash). Diaper rash is a common type of contact dermatitis that occurs where the skin has touched an irritant—urine, stool, or the diaper material itself.

Contact dermatitis causes redness, itching, and sometimes small bumps. You get the rash where you have touched an irritant, such as a chemical, or something you are allergic to (Source: NIH | Rash).

How Rashes Develop

Some rashes develop right away. Others form over several days. Although most rashes clear up fairly quickly, others are long-lasting and need long-term treatment (Source: NIH | Rash).

Because rashes can be caused by many different things, it's important to figure out what kind you have before starting treatment (Source: NIH | Rash).

Diaper Rash and Vomiting

Vomiting forces the contents of the stomach up through the food pipe and out of the mouth (Source: NIH | Vomiting). Common problems that may cause nausea and vomiting include infections of the stomach or bowels, such as the "stomach flu" (Source: NIH | Vomiting).

Nausea is feeling an urge to vomit—it is often called "being sick to your stomach" (Source: NIH | Vomiting).

Nausea and vomiting may be early warning signs of more serious medical problems, such as blockages (Source: NIH | Vomiting). If your baby cannot keep fluids down, or if vomiting is accompanied by severe pain, seek medical attention.

Diaper Rash and Nosebleed

A nosebleed is loss of blood from the tissue lining the nose. Bleeding most often occurs from one nostril only (Source: NIH | Nosebleed). Nosebleeds are very common. Most nosebleeds occur because of minor irritations of the inside of the nostrils due to colds (Source: NIH | Nosebleed).

The nose contains many small blood vessels that bleed easily. Air moving through the nose can dry and irritate the membranes lining the inside of the nose. Crusts can form that bleed when irritated. Nosebleeds occur more often in the winter, when cold viruses are common and indoor air tends to be drier (Source: NIH | Nosebleed).

Most nosebleeds occur on the front of the nasal septum—the piece of tissue that separates the two sides of the nose. This type of nosebleed can be easy for a trained professional to stop. Less commonly, nosebleeds may occur higher on the septum or deeper in the nose such as in the sinuses or the base (Source: NIH | Nosebleed).

Diaper Rash Treatment

Home care for diaper rash includes:

Frequent diaper changes – keep the area clean and dry

Gentle cleansing – use warm water and a soft cloth; avoid wipes with alcohol or fragrance

Air exposure – allow the diaper area to air dry completely before putting on a new diaper

Barrier creams – use diaper rash creams containing zinc oxide or petroleum jelly to protect the skin

Avoid irritants – stop using any wipes, diapers, or creams that seem to worsen the rash

When to Call the Doctor

Call your pediatrician if your baby's diaper rash:

Does not improve with home care within 2 to 3 days

Worsens despite treatment

Has blisters or raw skin – especially if oozing, crusting, or spreading

Is accompanied by fever – especially if the fever is very high

Is accompanied by vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down

Spreads beyond the diaper area – to the thighs, abdomen, or back

Is very painful – causing your baby to cry excessively during diaper changes

Emergency Signs Requiring Immediate Care

Seek emergency medical evaluation immediately if your baby's diaper rash is accompanied by:

Signs of dehydration – fewer wet diapers (less than 4 in 24 hours for infants), no tears when crying, sunken eyes, dry mouth, or lethargy

Difficulty breathing – rapid breathing, wheezing, grunting, or visible chest retractions

Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue – this could indicate a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

Purple or dark red spots that do not blanch (turn white) when pressed – this could indicate a serious infection such as meningitis

Confusion or unusual drowsiness – difficulty waking or acting abnormally

A stiff neck – this could indicate meningitis

A seizure

Green or bloody vomit – this may indicate a blockage (Source: NIH | Vomiting)

Nosebleed that will not stop after 20 minutes of applying pressure – this may require professional intervention (Source: NIH | Nosebleed)

Nosebleed following head trauma

High fever with a rash – this combination can indicate serious infections

Rash that spreads rapidly across the body

Blisters that burst and leave raw, weeping skin

Skin that feels warm to the touch and is accompanied by fever

Special Considerations for Infants

Any rash accompanied by fever in a baby under 3 months of age requires immediate medical attention. Their immune systems are still developing, and what might be a minor infection in an older child can be serious in a newborn. Even without other symptoms, a rash with fever in this age group should be evaluated by a health care provider right away.

When a Rash May Indicate a Serious Condition

Rashes accompanied by the following symptoms may indicate serious conditions:

Meningitis – rash that does not blanch when pressed, stiff neck, high fever, irritability

Severe allergic reaction – swelling of the face or tongue, difficulty breathing, hives

Bacterial infection – rash with high fever, lethargy, rapid breathing

Kawasaki disease – rash with fever lasting more than 5 days, red eyes, swollen hands and feet

Blockage – rash with green or bloody vomit (Source: NIH | Vomiting)

Persistent Symptoms

Contact your pediatrician if your baby has:

Rash that keeps coming back – this may indicate a chronic skin condition or allergy

Persistent vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down

Fever lasting more than 48 hours without an obvious cause

Frequent nosebleeds that recur without an obvious cause (Source: NIH | Nosebleed)
