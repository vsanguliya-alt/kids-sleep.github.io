Preschooler Sleep Apnea Signs: What Parents Need to Know

Sleep apnea is a common disorder that causes your breathing to stop or get very shallow during sleep . The most common type is obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which happens when the airway becomes narrowed or collapses during sleep, blocking the flow of air . While it is often associated with adults, sleep apnea can also affect young children, and it's important for parents to recognize the signs.

What Are the Signs of Sleep Apnea in Preschoolers?

The symptoms of sleep apnea in children can appear both during the night and during the day.

Nighttime Signs

Loud, habitual snoring – this is often the most noticeable symptom. Not everyone who snores has sleep apnea, but frequent, loud snoring is a common warning sign .

Pauses in breathing – a parent may observe their child stop breathing for 10 seconds or more during sleep, followed by a gasp, snort, or body jerk as they struggle to breathe .

Restless sleep – the child may toss and turn frequently, kick, or sleep in unusual positions .

Night sweats – due to the extra effort required to breathe.

Bedwetting – which may occur even if the child has previously been dry at night.

Sleepwalking or other parasomnias.

Daytime Signs

Morning headaches – due to a buildup of carbon dioxide and lack of oxygen overnight.

Daytime sleepiness – the child may be drowsy, take naps when they normally wouldn't, or seem lethargic and tired during the day .

Mouth breathing and a dry mouth upon waking.

Behavioral issues – the child may appear irritable, moody, or hyperactive. In fact, children with sleep apnea are often misdiagnosed with ADHD because the symptoms overlap .

Learning and attention problems – they may have trouble concentrating, remembering things, or staying focused at school .

Slow growth – in some cases, sleep apnea can affect growth due to disrupted sleep and the body's production of growth hormones.

Why Is This Happening?

Sleep apnea in children is most often caused by enlarged tonsils and adenoids (tissue at the back of the throat) . Other risk factors include obesity, having a small jaw, or having a family history of sleep apnea . In some cases, underlying conditions like Down syndrome can also increase the risk . A sleep study is often needed to confirm the diagnosis and assess the severity.

When to See a Doctor

If you notice any of these signs—especially loud snoring combined with observed pauses in breathing or daytime symptoms—it is important to discuss them with your pediatrician. Treatment can include lifestyle changes, a CPAP machine, or, often, removal of the tonsils and adenoids .
