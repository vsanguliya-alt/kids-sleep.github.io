Snoring in Your 4-Year-Old: When to Worry

Snoring in children, particularly preschoolers, can be a source of anxiety for parents. While occasional snoring is often harmless, loud and persistent snoring can be a sign of an underlying issue that needs attention.

The Common Causes

The most frequent cause of snoring and sleep-disordered breathing in children is enlarged tonsils and adenoids. These are pads of tissue at the back of the throat and nose that can grow large enough to partially block the airway during sleep. Other factors such as allergies causing nasal congestion, asthma, obesity, or a deviated septum can also contribute to snoring.

Normal vs. Concerning Snoring

It is estimated that about 1 in 10 children snore regularly. Occasional, soft snoring—often triggered by a cold or seasonal allergies—is common and usually resolves on its own and isn&#x27;t a cause for concern.

However, chronic snoring is different. Snoring that happens 4 or more nights per week is considered chronic and is not benign; it warrants a medical evaluation.

When to See a Doctor: Signs of Sleep Apnea

Chronic, loud snoring can be the primary symptom of pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). This condition occurs when a child&#x27;s breathing is partially or completely blocked during sleep, causing pauses in breathing that can occur many times a night.

See your child&#x27;s healthcare professional if your child has any of the following symptoms of sleep apnea:

Nighttime signs: Frequent, loud snoring, noticeable pauses in breathing followed by gasping or snorting, restless sleep, mouth breathing, and bedwetting.

Daytime signs: Morning headaches, mouth breathing during the day, difficulty paying attention or learning, behavioral issues such as hyperactivity or aggression, and daytime sleepiness (though children are more likely to show hyperactivity than drowsiness).

When to Seek Immediate Attention

While snoring is usually a chronic concern, you should seek immediate medical attention if your child experiences apnea (pauses in breathing) lasting longer than 10 seconds or if they ever turn blue or stop breathing. This is a medical emergency.

Next Steps

If your 4-year-old has been snoring loudly and persistently, especially if accompanied by any of the daytime symptoms above, it&#x27;s important to speak with your pediatrician. They can assess your child for enlarged tonsils and adenoids and determine if a referral to a specialist is needed.
