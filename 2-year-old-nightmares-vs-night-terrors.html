Nightmares vs. Night Terrors in 2-Year-Olds: A Parent's Guide

At age 2, your toddler may begin experiencing either nightmares or night terrors—two distinct sleep events that can be confusing and alarming for parents. Understanding the difference is key to knowing how to respond.

🌙 Nightmares: Bad Dreams They Remember

A nightmare is a frightening dream that happens during REM (dreaming) sleep, typically in the second half of the night or early morning . Children can start having nightmares as young as 6 months of age .

During a nightmare, your child will:

Wake up fully, feeling afraid and often crying

Recognize you and seek comfort

Be able to describe parts of the scary dream (if they have the language skills)

Have difficulty falling back asleep

How to help: Respond quickly, reassure them that they are safe, remind them that dreams are not real, and allow a nightlight if it helps them feel better .

🌑 Night Terrors: Confusing Sleep Episodes They Won't Remember

A night terror (or sleep terror) is a type of parasomnia—a disruptive sleep behavior—that occurs during deep, non-REM sleep, usually in the first 1–3 hours after falling asleep .

During a night terror, your child will:

Sit up in bed, scream, or cry uncontrollably

Look terrified with a glassy-eyed stare but not recognize you

Sweat, breathe rapidly, and have a racing heartbeat

Thrash or kick

Be very difficult or impossible to wake

Not respond to your comfort—they may even push you away

Fall back into deep sleep after 5–15 minutes

Have no memory of the event the next morning

A study in JAMA Pediatrics found that sleep terrors are most common around age 1.5 years (18 months) and can affect children for several years . About 35% of toddlers around this age experience them, and they can persist until age 3 .

Common triggers include: being overtired, stress, fever, illness, new medications, or sleeping in an unfamiliar environment .

✅ What to Do During a Night Terror

Do NOT try to wake your child—this can confuse and frighten them more . Instead:

Stay calm and stay with them

Gently guide them back to bed if they get up

Ensure their safety (block stairs, lock windows)

Wait patiently; it usually passes within minutes

🩺 When to See a Doctor

Occasional night terrors are not usually a cause for concern . However, contact your pediatrician if night terrors:

Occur often or regularly

Disrupt sleep for the family

Lead to safety concerns or injury

Result in daytime sleepiness

Are accompanied by snoring or pauses in breathing (may indicate sleep apnea)

Most children outgrow night terrors by their teenage years .
