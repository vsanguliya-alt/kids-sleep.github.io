Why Won&#x27;t My 18-Month-Old Sleep Alone? Understanding Separation Anxiety

If your 18-month-old suddenly refuses to sleep alone, you are not alone. This is a very common challenge at this age, and the primary culprit is often a normal developmental stage called separation anxiety.

Separation Anxiety: The Root of the Problem

Separation anxiety peaks between 10 and 18 months of age . At around 18 months, toddlers start to develop a stronger sense of self, which comes hand-in-hand with a fear of being separated from their parents . They may become more clingy, crying or calling out when you leave the room or put them to bed .

This can cause:

Refusing to Sleep Alone: The simple act of you leaving can cause real distress .

Frequent Night Wakings: Your toddler may wake up and need reassurance that you are still nearby .

Bedtime Battles: Resistance to going to sleep, with increased stalling, protests, and tantrums .

How to Help Your Toddler Sleep Alone

Here are some gentle, evidence-based strategies to help your toddler through this phase:

Stick to a Predictable Routine: A consistent, calming bedtime routine is your best tool. It provides a sense of security and predictability when your child is feeling anxious .

Provide a Comfort Object: A special soft toy or blanket can act as a &quot;transitional object,&quot; offering comfort and a sense of security when you are not there .

Acknowledge Their Fear: Validate their feelings. If they are scared of a monster, check the room together (with the lights off) and prove it&#x27;s safe . Use a small, dim night-light to help reduce any fear of the dark .

Practice Being Apart: During the day, practice short separations. Tell your child you are going to another room and will be right back. Always return as promised to build trust .

Be Calm and Reassuring: If they call for you, go in and offer comfort, but try to make your visits brief and calm. Avoid creating new habits, like getting into bed with them . You can pat their back, tell them it&#x27;s time to sleep, and leave them awake in their bed .

When to Seek Help

While this phase is normal, the Cedars-Sinai medical center notes that if the symptoms are severe and last longer than 4 weeks, it could be a sign of a more significant separation anxiety disorder (SAD) . If you are worried, speak to your pediatrician.
