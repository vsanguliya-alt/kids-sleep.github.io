Sleep Regression in Children: A Phoenix Parent's Guide

If your child, who was once sleeping through the night, is suddenly waking up frequently or fighting bedtime, you may be dealing with a sleep regression. This is a temporary phase where a child's sleep patterns become disrupted. The good news is that it's a normal part of development and typically lasts only a few weeks .

What Causes Sleep Regression?

Understanding why sleep regressions happen can help you respond with patience. Common causes include developmental milestones, which can be so exciting that your baby prefers practicing new skills over sleeping . Teething pain or illness can cause discomfort that leads to night waking . Separation anxiety is often behind sleep regressions around 9 months of age, when babies become more aware of your absence . Growth spurts, changes in routine, and even stress from events like travel or starting daycare can all contribute to sleep disruptions .

Common Sleep Regression Ages

While sleep regressions can happen at any time, they are commonly seen around certain developmental milestones, including:

4 months: Often considered the "true" sleep regression, this occurs as a baby's sleep cycles begin to mature .

6 months, 9 months, 12 months, 18 months, and 2 years: These are also common ages for sleep regressions to appear .

These regressions usually last two to six weeks, depending on the child .

How to Manage Sleep Regression

A consistent and calming bedtime routine is key to helping your child settle. Stick to your routine as much as possible, as predictability provides comfort and security. For older babies and toddlers, you can start gentle sleep training to help them learn to fall back asleep independently, which is crucial for returning to sleep after night wakings . Pay attention to your child's sleep cues and be sure they are getting enough daytime sleep. Sometimes, an older baby or toddler might be ready to drop a nap, which can temporarily affect nighttime sleep .

When to See a Doctor

A sleep regression is normal, but you should talk to your pediatrician if you suspect there is an underlying problem. It's worth a visit if your child's sleep disturbances persist for longer than a few weeks, or if they are accompanied by concerning symptoms like loud, disruptive snoring or gasping for air during sleep, which could be a sign of sleep apnea . About 1% to 4% of children have obstructive sleep apnea, which can cause issues like night waking and restless sleep .
