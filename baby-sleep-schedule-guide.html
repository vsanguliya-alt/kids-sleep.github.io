Baby Sleep Schedule in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

A consistent, age-appropriate sleep schedule is one of the most important foundations for your child's health, development, and wellbeing. While every child is unique, understanding typical sleep needs and evidence-based strategies can help you build a routine that works for your family.

How Much Sleep Does Your Child Need?

Sleep needs change dramatically during the first five years of life. According to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the recommended sleep ranges are :

Newborns (0months): 14hours per 24-hour period

Infants (4months): 12hours per 24-hour period

Toddlers (1years): 11hours per 24-hour period

Preschoolers (3years): 10hours per 24-hour period

These totals include naps. The AAP also reports that one in four children under age five does not get the recommended amount of sleep, which can lead to challenges in behavior, learning, and health .

Newborn Sleep (0Months)

Newborns sleep in short stretches of 1hours, waking frequently to feed . Their internal body clock is not yet developed, so sleep happens throughout the day and night . Newborns may sleep up to 17 hours a day and stay awake only 1hours at a time .

Normal newborn sleep signs:

Waking every 2hours to feed

Irregular sleep patterns

Short sleep cycles of 45minutes When to start a schedule: Focus on responding to your baby's sleepy cues (eye rubbing, fussiness, yawning) rather than a strict schedule . Around 4weeks, circadian rhythms begin to emerge, and you can start helping your baby differentiate day from night .

Infant Sleep (4Months)

By 4 months, many babies begin developing more predictable sleep patterns and may sleep up to 8hours at night . Between 6months, most children will sleep 10hours at night . Infants typically take 1naps per day, each lasting 30 minutes to 2 hours .

Key milestones:

At 4 months: 14 hours average per day, longer nighttime sleep begins At 6 months: most children sleep through the night By 9 months: most infants can sleep 8hours without a nighttime feeding Sample schedule for a 6-month-old:

7:00 AM: Wake, feed

9:00 AM: Nap (1hours)

12:00 PM: Feed

1:30 PM: Nap (1hours)

4:00 PM: Short nap (30mins)

7:00 PM: Bedtime routine begins

7:30 PM: Sleep

Toddler Sleep (1Years)

Toddlers typically need 11hours of good quality sleep every 24 hours, including naps, with consistent sleep and wake-up times . A one-year-old needs 11hours of good quality sleep every 24 hours, with 10hours at night and 1.5 hours of daytime naps .

Nap transitions:

Your toddler's two naps will likely reduce to one nap between 12 and 18 months By around 18 months, most children only need one nap each day By three years of age, the daytime nap is reducing and then stops Sample schedule for a 2-year-old:

7:00 AM: Wake

12:30 PM: Nap (1hours)

7:30 PM: Bedtime routine begins

8:00 PM: Sleep

Bedtime challenges: Toddlers may resist bedtime due to independence seeking and boundary testing . Waking at night peaks between 18 months and 2 years and then decreases over time .

Preschooler Sleep (3Years)

Preschoolers need 10hours of good quality sleep every 24 hours with consistent sleep and wake-up times . By 3years, many preschoolers begin dropping their daytime nap. Even if they don't sleep, quiet rest time during the day can still help .

Sample schedule for a 4-year-old:

7:00 AM: Wake

1:00 PM: Quiet time (30mins)

8:00 PM: Bedtime routine begins

8:30 PM: Sleep

Evidence-Based Strategies for Healthy Sleep

1. Create a Consistent Bedtime Routine

A predictable, calming bedtime routine helps children feel secure and ready for sleep . This can include a warm bath, brushing teeth, reading a story, and quiet cuddling. Keep the routine to a set amount of time each night (typically 20minutes) .

2. Put Your Child to Bed Awake

It's important for children to be put to bed awake so they learn to fall asleep on their own . This helps them develop self-soothing skills and reduces nighttime wake-ups .

3. Follow the ABCs of Safe Sleep

The AAP recommends the ABCs of safe baby sleep for infants under 1 year :

A is for Alone. Put your baby to sleep alone in their crib. Keep soft items like toys, crib bumpers, and blankets out of the crib .

B is for Back. Place your baby on their back to sleep for all sleep times—naps and at night .

C is for Crib. Use a firm, flat sleep surface, such as a mattress in a safety-approved crib covered only by a fitted sheet .

4. Room Share, Don't Bed Share

Keep your baby's sleep area in the same room where you sleep for at least the first 6 months . Room sharing can decrease the risk of SIDS by as much as 50% and is much safer than bed sharing .

5. Avoid Screens Before Bed

Put away any screens (TV, smartphones, tablets, computers) during quiet time . Screen time is stimulating and not relaxing, making it harder for children to fall asleep .

6. Watch for Tired Signs

Watching for tired signs helps prevent overtiredness, which makes settling more difficult . Signs include:

Newborns: pulling at ears, jerking arms and legs, or sucking fingers Babies and toddlers: grizzling, demanding attention, or losing interest in play When to Talk to Your Pediatrician

Contact your pediatrician if:

Your child has consistent trouble falling or staying asleep

You notice loud snoring or pauses in breathing during sleep

Daytime sleepiness interferes with daily activities

You have concerns about your child's development or behavior related to sleep

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my child's sleep?

Call your pediatrician if your child has trouble breathing, snores loudly, has pauses in breathing during sleep, or shows signs of extreme daytime sleepiness that affects daily activities.

Q: How long does a sleep regression normally last?

Most sleep regressions last between two and six weeks. Maintaining a consistent routine and responding to your child with comfort can help them get back on track.

Q: What home remedies help with sleep problems?

Establish a consistent bedtime routine, avoid screens for at least an hour before bed, and create a dark, quiet sleep environment. For infants, room sharing (separate surface) can reduce SIDS risk by 50%.

Q: Is it normal for my child to wake up at night?

Yes. Waking at night is normal, especially for infants who need to feed. By 6 months, most children can sleep through the night, but regressions and developmental leaps can cause temporary disruptions.

Q: Does teething cause sleep problems?

Yes, the pain and discomfort from teething are a common cause of sleep disturbances and regressions in infants and toddlers.

Q: When should I go to the ER for a sleep issue?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has trouble breathing, turns blue or grey, or is very difficult to wake. These are emergency signs.
