Baby Sleep Schedule in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Establishing a healthy sleep schedule is one of the most important foundations for your child's development. Quality sleep supports learning, memory, emotional regulation, and physical growth . This guide provides evidence-based recommendations on how much sleep your child needs, how to build a consistent routine, and what to do when sleep problems arise.

How Much Sleep Does Your Child Need?

Sleep needs change dramatically during the first five years of life. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), provides the following recommended sleep ranges per 24-hour period :

Age Recommended Sleep (24 hours)

Newborn (0–3 months) 14–17 hours

Infant (4–12 months) 12–16 hours

Toddler (1–2 years) 11–14 hours

Preschooler (3–5 years) 10–13 hours

These totals include naps. Newborns sleep in short stretches of 2–4 hours at a time as their internal body clock is still developing . By around 4 months, many infants begin developing more predictable sleep patterns and may sleep longer stretches at night .

Building a Consistent Bedtime Routine

A predictable, calming bedtime routine is the single most effective tool for helping your child sleep well . It signals to your child's brain that it's time to wind down and prepares them for rest.

Sample Bedtime Routine (20–30 minutes)

Dim the lights to signal that sleep is approaching .

Warm bath – helps relax muscles and lower body temperature.

Put on pajamas and brush teeth.

Read a book or sing a lullaby – quiet, screen-free activities.

Cuddle and say goodnight – put your child to bed drowsy but awake .

Key tips for success:

Keep bedtimes and wake times consistent, even on weekends .

End all screen time at least 1–2 hours before bed .

Keep the bedroom cool, dark, and quiet .

Offer a comfort object (like a small blanket or stuffed animal) for older children.

Safe Sleep Guidelines for Infants

For babies under 1 year, following safe sleep guidelines is essential to reduce the risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) and accidental suffocation. The AAP and CDC recommend the ABCs of safe sleep :

A – Alone: Babies should sleep alone in their own crib, bassinet, or play yard. Never sleep with your baby .

B – Back: Always place your baby on their back to sleep, for both naps and nighttime .

C – Crib: Use a firm, flat, safety-approved sleep surface with a fitted sheet. Keep soft objects, loose bedding, pillows, and toys out of the crib .

Additional recommendations:

Room-share (not bed-share) for at least the first 6 months to reduce SIDS risk by up to 50% .

Avoid smoking during pregnancy and around your baby .

Offer a pacifier at nap time and bedtime .

Signs Your Child May Have a Sleep Problem

While some night waking is normal, persistent difficulties may indicate an underlying issue. Contact your pediatrician if your child regularly:

Snores loudly, gasps, or chokes during sleep – this could indicate sleep apnea (affects 1–4% of children) .

Has pauses in breathing while asleep .

Is excessively sleepy during the day .

Has frequent nightmares or night terrors that disrupt sleep .

Has secondary bedwetting (after being dry for 6 months) .

Has difficulty waking up in the morning or appears groggy .

Seek emergency care immediately if your child has trouble breathing, turns blue or grey, or is very difficult to wake.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my child's sleep?

Call your pediatrician if your child snores loudly, has pauses in breathing during sleep, is excessively sleepy during the day, or has frequent night terrors or bedwetting after being dry. Seek emergency care if they have trouble breathing or are very difficult to wake.

Q: How can I help my child fall asleep independently?

Put your child to bed drowsy but awake . This helps them learn to fall asleep on their own, so they can resettle themselves if they wake during the night .

Q: What home remedies help with sleep problems?

Maintain a consistent bedtime routine, avoid screens before bed, keep the room dark and quiet, and offer a comfort object . For babies, room-sharing (separate surface) can reduce SIDS risk by 50% .

Q: Is it normal for my newborn to wake frequently at night?

Yes. Newborns have small stomachs and need to feed every 2–4 hours, so waking at night is normal and expected .

Q: When should I move my child from a crib to a bed?

This varies, but many children transition between 2 and 3 years old. Signs include climbing out of the crib or showing readiness. Keep the new bed safe with side rails if needed.

Q: Can teething cause sleep problems?

Yes, teething discomfort can disrupt sleep. Offer a cool teething ring or consult your pediatrician about pain relief if needed.
