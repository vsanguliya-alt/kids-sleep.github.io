6-Month Sleep Regression: What It Is and How to Handle It

If your 6-month-old, who was sleeping well, suddenly starts waking up frequently at night, you are likely dealing with a sleep regression. This is a temporary and normal phase where a baby who previously slept consistently experiences sleep disruptions, including more frequent night wakings, trouble falling back asleep, increased fussiness, or changes in nap schedules .

Why Is This Happening?

This regression is not a sign that something is wrong, but rather a signal that your baby is hitting major developmental milestones . Several factors often converge around this age:

New Physical Skills: Your baby may be learning to roll over, sit up, or push up on their hands. The excitement of practicing these new skills can disrupt their sleep .

Increased Awareness: Babies become more aware of their surroundings and may have trouble settling because they'd rather stay awake and interact with the world .

Separation Anxiety: At this age, babies start to develop "object permanence"—they realize you exist even when you're not there. This can cause them to become unsettled when you leave the room, leading to more night wakings .

Teething: Emerging teeth can cause discomfort that interrupts sleep. While not every baby starts teething at six months, it's a common age to begin .

Sleep Environment or Routine Changes: Even small shifts, like a vacation or starting solids, can affect your baby's sleep .

Sleep Associations: All babies wake briefly during the night. If your baby is accustomed to being rocked or fed to sleep, they may struggle to fall back asleep on their own after these brief wakings .

How Long Does It Last?

The duration of a 6-month sleep regression can vary. While it typically lasts from a few days to a few weeks , some sources note it can last up to two to four weeks . In some cases, it may persist for two to six weeks . If it lasts longer, it may be worth consulting your pediatrician .

Tips for Managing the Regression

While you cannot prevent the regression, you can implement strategies to help your baby and yourself get through it:

Establish a Consistent Bedtime Routine: A calm, predictable routine (like a bath, a book, or a lullaby) signals to your baby that sleep is approaching. Keeping the routine consistent is a strong recommendation from pediatric experts .

Practice Healthy Sleep Habits: Put your baby down drowsy but awake. This helps them learn to fall asleep independently, which is a crucial skill for getting back to sleep after night wakings .

Try Sleep Training: This involves teaching your baby to fall asleep on their own. One recommended method involves gradually increasing how long you wait to respond to your crying baby, helping them learn to self-soothe .

Wait Before Responding: When your baby wakes, don’t rush in. You can watch on a monitor to see if they can settle themselves back to sleep after a minute or two .

Keep Nighttime Interactions Calm: If you need to attend to your baby at night, keep the interaction quiet and business-like, avoiding stimulating activity or play .

When to See a Doctor

While sleep regression is a normal part of development, it's best to consult your pediatrician if the sleep problems persist for more than a few weeks, if you are concerned about your baby's eating or behavior, or if the disruptions are accompanied by other signs of illness .
