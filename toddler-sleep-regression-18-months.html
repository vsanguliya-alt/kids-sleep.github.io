Understanding the 18-Month Sleep Regression

What Is the 18-Month Sleep Regression?

The 18-month sleep regression is a common phase where a toddler who previously slept well suddenly starts waking at night, resisting naps, or fighting bedtime. This is a normal developmental stage, not a sign that you've done something wrong.

Why Does It Happen?

At around 18 months, toddlers experience major developmental leaps. They are learning new words, walking more confidently, developing a stronger sense of independence, and often experiencing separation anxiety. These exciting changes can make it harder for them to "power down" at night.

How Much Sleep Does an 18-Month-Old Need?

The amount of sleep a child needs depends on their age, lifestyle, health, and sleep patterns (Source: NIH | Healthy Sleep). Children aged 1 to 2 years generally need 11 to 14 hours of sleep per day, including naps (Source: NIH | Healthy Sleep). This sleep is essential for restoring energy, supporting learning and memory, and keeping them healthy (Source: NIH | Healthy Sleep).

During sleep, the brain cycles through different stages, including light and deep sleep, which are all necessary for healthy development (Source: NIH | Healthy Sleep).

How to Manage the Regression

Stick to a consistent routine. Children thrive on predictability. A regular bedtime routine—like a bath, brushing teeth, reading a story, and saying goodnight—helps signal that it's time to sleep.

Keep calm and be reassuring. If your toddler wakes at night, respond calmly and quickly. A quick cuddle or some gentle words can help them feel secure without creating new sleep habits.

Maintain age-appropriate boundaries. It's okay to offer comfort, but try to avoid bringing your toddler into your bed unless that is a long-term plan for your family.

When Will It End?

The 18-month sleep regression typically lasts 2 to 6 weeks, though it varies from child to child. Consistency and patience are key. If sleep problems persist or you have concerns about your child's health, talk to your pediatrician.
