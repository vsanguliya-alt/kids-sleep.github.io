When Should You Worry About a 1-Year-Old Who Won't Sleep Alone?

It is common for 1-year-olds to resist sleeping alone. This is often a normal developmental phase related to separation anxiety and newfound independence. However, there are times when this behavior may signal an underlying issue.

When Is It Normal?

At this age, a child's growing awareness of their surroundings and their attachment to parents often make them want to be close at night. This is a typical milestone and not usually a cause for concern. It is a phase that many toddlers go through as they learn to self-soothe and feel secure.

When Should You Worry? (Red Flags)

You should be concerned if your child's inability to sleep alone is accompanied by any of the following signs:

Signs of illness: Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, or severe pain can cause sleep disruption.

Breathing problems: Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, gasping, or choking sounds during sleep (Source: NIH | Sleep Apnea | MedlinePlus – Sleep Apnea).

Extreme distress: Panic attacks, which involve sudden, overwhelming anxiety and fear, can interfere with sleep (Source: NIH | Panic Disorder: MedlinePlus – Panic Disorder).

Daytime dysfunction: Extreme fatigue, irritability, or behavior problems that affect daytime functioning.

Regression: A return to behaviors they had outgrown, such as severe separation anxiety, which may be a sign of stress.

Frequent night wakings: Waking up multiple times a night and refusing to go back to sleep without a parent.

If you notice these red flags, schedule a visit with your pediatrician to rule out medical or behavioral issues.

Safe Sleep Guidelines for a 1-Year-Old

While your child is in a crib, it is crucial to follow safe sleep guidelines to reduce the risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) and other sleep-related causes of infant death (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus). The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends:

Placing your baby on their back to sleep, even for short naps. "Tummy time" is for when babies are awake and someone is watching (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus).

Having your baby sleep in your room for at least the first six months. Your baby should sleep close to you, but on a separate surface designed for infants, such as a crib or bassinet (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus).

Using a firm sleep surface, such as a crib mattress covered with a fitted sheet (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus).

Keeping soft objects and loose bedding away from your baby's sleep area (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus).

Making sure that your baby doesn't get too hot (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus).

What to Do

For a child without red flags, a consistent and reassuring bedtime routine can help. This includes a calm bath, reading a story, and singing a lullaby. If your child consistently struggles, consult your pediatrician for guidance on behavioral strategies.

The Bottom Line

While it is normal for a 1-year-old to not want to sleep alone, you should worry if the behavior is sudden, severe, or accompanied by other symptoms like breathing issues or extreme distress. Otherwise, it is likely a normal phase that will improve with time and consistent routines.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for my 1-year-old not sleeping alone?

Call your pediatrician if your child shows signs of illness, has breathing problems like loud snoring or gasping, or if their sleep problems affect daytime behavior and mood.

Q: How long does it take for a 1-year-old to sleep alone?

This varies widely. It can take a few days to several weeks of consistent routine. If the problem persists for more than a month, it’s a good idea to discuss it with your pediatrician.

Q: What home remedies help a 1-year-old sleep alone?

Establish a calm and consistent bedtime routine, offer a lovey or security object (if safe), and provide reassurance through a brief check-in if they wake.

Q: Is it normal for a 1-year-old to wake up and cry at night?

Yes, it is common. If they are not in pain or distress, a brief check-in to reassure them without picking them up is often recommended. If they are sick or in pain, attend to their needs.

Q: Does co-sleeping reduce SIDS risk?

No. The AAP recommends room-sharing (sleeping in the same room, but on a separate surface) for at least the first six months to reduce the risk of SIDS. Bed-sharing is not recommended (Source: NIH | Sudden Infant Death Syndrome: MedlinePlus).

Q: When should I go to the ER for a 1-year-old not sleeping alone?

Go to the ER if your child has trouble breathing, turns blue or grey, or is difficult to wake. These are emergency signs. For other concerns, contact your pediatrician.
