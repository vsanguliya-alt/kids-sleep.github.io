Toddler Cosleeping Safety: 7 Signs Parents Should Know

Cosleeping, or sharing a bed with your toddler, is a personal decision with important safety considerations. While the practice is common in some cultures, health organizations like the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) strongly advise against bed-sharing due to significant safety risks. Here are the signs that indicate whether your cosleeping arrangement may be unsafe.

1. Your Toddler is Under 4 Months Old

The risk of sleep-related death increases when sharing a sleep surface with an infant under 4 months of age. For children in this age group, any form of bed-sharing is highly discouraged.

2. Your Child was Born Prematurely or with Low Birth Weight

Premature infants and those with low birth weight are at an elevated baseline risk for Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS). Bed-sharing is generally not recommended for these babies.

3. The Sleep Surface is Soft

Adult beds, couches, armchairs, and mattresses that are not firm (such as memory foam or waterbeds) increase the risk of suffocation and entrapment.

4. There is Loose Bedding, Pillows, or Soft Objects

Pillows, blankets, quilts, and other soft bedding can cover a child's face, leading to suffocation. The sleep area should be clear of these items.

5. There is Risk of Entrapment or Falls

Your toddler could get trapped between the mattress and a wall, headboard, or other object. They could also roll or fall out of bed and sustain an injury.

6. Someone in the Bed has Been Drinking Alcohol, Using Drugs, or is a Smoker

Parental smoking is one of the most strongly established risk factors for SIDS. Alcohol and drug use can impair a parent's ability to wake up and respond to the child, dramatically increasing the risk of accidental harm.

7. Other Children or Pets Share the Bed

Children or pets should never share the same sleep surface with a baby or toddler. They can accidentally roll onto the child or cause other hazards.

Safer Alternatives and Red Flags to Watch For

The AAP recommends room-sharing without bed-sharing for at least the first six months, and ideally for the entire first year. This means your toddler sleeps in their own crib, bassinet, or play yard in the same room as you, which can reduce the risk of SIDS by up to 50%.

If you are worried about your toddler's health or safety, call your health care provider right away. You should also seek emergency care if your child is having difficulty breathing or shows any other concerning symptoms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about cosleeping safety?

Call your pediatrician if you have questions about safe sleep practices for your child’s age and health status. Also, seek emergency care if your child is having difficulty breathing, is unconscious, or you have any urgent safety concerns.

Q: How long does a sleep regression normally last?

Sleep regressions typically last between one and three weeks. If sleep disturbances persist longer or are accompanied by signs of illness, consult your pediatrician.

Q: What home remedies help with toddler sleep problems?

The best approach is to maintain a consistent, calming bedtime routine, ensure the sleep environment is safe and comfortable, and avoid screens before bed.

Q: Is co-sleeping a sign of a developmental issue?

No, the desire to co-sleep can be a normal part of a toddler's development. However, it is important to prioritize safe sleep practices.

Q: Does teething cause sleep disturbances?

Yes, the pain and discomfort from teething are a common cause of sleep disturbances in toddlers, but they do not make cosleeping safer.
