Nightmares vs. Night Terrors in 1-Year-Olds

Many parents confuse nightmares and night terrors, but they are very different sleep events. Understanding the distinction can help you respond appropriately and reassure you that these episodes, though frightening to witness, are usually not a cause for concern. This guide explains the key differences and how to handle each.

Key Differences at a Glance

The most important distinction is that a nightmare is a bad dream that a child wakes up from and can often remember, while a night terror is a partial arousal from deep sleep where the child appears terrified but remains asleep and does not remember the event .

Here is a quick comparison based on information from pediatric sleep specialists and health authorities :

Feature Nightmare Night Terror

Sleep Stage Occurs during REM (dreaming) sleep . Occurs during non-REM (deep) sleep .

What Do They Look Like?

Night Terrors: Signs to Watch For

During a night terror, your 1-year-old may:

Sit up in bed and scream or cry .

Thrash around and seem very frightened and confused .

Breathe very fast, sweat, and have a rapid heart rate .

Stare with wide-open eyes but not recognize you or respond to you .

This episode typically lasts from a few minutes up to 15minutes, and then the child will calm down and go back to sleep .

Nightmares: What to Expect

A nightmare is simply a bad dream. Your child will:

Wake up fully crying or appearing scared .

Seek comfort and be soothed by your presence .

Be able to remember at least part of the scary dream .

How to Respond

During a Night Terror:

Stay calm and stay nearby to ensure your child’s safety .

Do NOT try to wake them. Trying to wake a child during a night terror can prolong the episode or disorient them .

Clear the area of dangerous objects. If needed, gently guide them back to bed .

During a Nightmare:

Reassure and comfort your child with a cuddle and soothing words .

Stay calm and help them feel safe to go back to sleep in their own bed .

Talk about it the next day if they want to, to help them process the dream, but avoid scary stories or movies before bed .

When to Call the Doctor

Occasional nightmares and night terrors are normal, but speak with your pediatrician if :

Night terrors occur more than twice a week .

Episodes last longer than 30 minutes .

The episodes are disrupting sleep on a regular basis or affecting daily functioning .

Your child is sleepwalking or shows other concerning symptoms like drooling, stiffening, or jerking during an episode .

Night terrors continue after the age of 12 .
