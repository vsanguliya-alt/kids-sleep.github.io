Child Nap Schedule: A Guide for Parents

Naps are a critical part of your child's development, providing essential rest for their growing brain and body. A consistent nap schedule can help ensure your child gets the sleep they need to thrive.

Why Naps Matter

Naps are not just for parents to have a break—they play a crucial role in your child's cognitive development, emotional regulation, and physical growth. Well-rested children nap more consistently, and a good daytime sleep routine supports learning and memory consolidation .

Child Nap Schedule by Age

As your child grows, their sleep needs change significantly. Here is a general guide to what you can expect at each stage.

4 to 12 Months

During this period, babies typically need 2 to 3 naps per day. A typical pattern is a mid-morning nap and an early afternoon nap, with some babies also needing a late-afternoon catnap . By 9 months, most babies are on a consistent two-nap schedule . Babies over 9 months of age should not sleep after 3:30 PM, as it can lead to a later bedtime .

12 to 18 Months

After their first birthday, most children transition to one nap per day, typically in the middle of the day . By about 18 months, many toddlers are on a single afternoon nap lasting 1 to 2 hours . A typical schedule for a toddler this age might be:

Time Activity

7:00 AM Wake up

12:00 PM Start of nap

2:00 PM Wake from nap

7:00 PM Start of bedtime routine

7:30 PM Bedtime

2 to 3 Years

Children usually continue to nap until around age 3 . At 2 years old, a child typically needs about 11 to 14 hours of sleep in a 24-hour period, with a nap of 1 to 3 hours . If your child is sleeping well and seems content with a different schedule, it is likely fine for them.

Tips for a Successful Nap Schedule

Watch for tired cues: Yawning, eye-rubbing, and fussiness are signs it's time for a nap.

Create a consistent routine: A short, calming pre-nap routine (like a story or lullaby) in a dark, quiet room helps signal that it's time to sleep .

Put your child down drowsy but awake: This helps them learn to self-soothe.

Be flexible: If your child is unwell or going through a developmental leap, their sleep needs may vary.

Trust your instincts. If you are worried about your child's sleep, call your health care provider right away.
