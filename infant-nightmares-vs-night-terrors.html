Nightmares vs. Night Terrors in Infants: A Parent&#x27;s Guide

Understanding Infant Sleep Disturbances

For parents of very young infants, the terms &quot;nightmares&quot; and &quot;night terrors&quot; can be confusing and worrying. However, it&#x27;s important to understand that true nightmares and night terrors are not common in infants. They are sleep phenomena that typically emerge in toddlers and older children. Night terrors are most common in children between 3 and 7 years old .

What Infants Experience Instead

While a 6-month-old might wake up crying or appear distressed, this is usually not a nightmare or a night terror. Instead, infants often wake due to:

Hunger: Young babies have small stomachs and need to feed frequently .

Discomfort: Wet diapers, being too hot or too cold, or teething pain .

Need for comfort: The need for a parent&#x27;s presence to feel secure and fall back asleep .

Sleep cycle transitions: Waking briefly between sleep cycles, which is normal .

Understanding Sleep Terrors (Night Terrors)

Night terrors are a type of parasomnia—a disruptive sleep disorder—that occurs during deep, non-REM sleep, usually in the first part of the night, often between midnight and 2 a.m. . They are not dreams and the child is not awake during the episode .

During a night terror, a child will:

Scream, shout, or cry uncontrollably

Sit up in bed with a wide-eyed, terrified stare

Sweat, breathe heavily, and have a racing pulse

Be hard or impossible to wake up

Not respond to comfort or soothing

Have no memory of the event the next morning

Common triggers include: serious lack of sleep, stress, fever, schedule changes, and sleep interruptions .

Understanding Nightmares

A nightmare is a scary dream that occurs during REM (dreaming) sleep, typically in the later part of the night or early morning . Children often wake up fully from a nightmare and remember it in detail.

During a nightmare, a child will:

Wake up scared and upset

Be able to describe the dream afterward

Seek comfort from you and respond to reassurance

Often go back to sleep after being comforted

When to Call the Doctor

Occasional night terrors are not usually a cause for concern . However, contact your pediatrician if night terrors:

Happen often (more than twice a week)

Regularly disrupt the family&#x27;s sleep

Lead to safety concerns or injury

Result in daytime symptoms of extreme sleepiness

Continue beyond the teen years or start after age 5

Are accompanied by snoring or pauses in breathing (may indicate sleep apnea)

Most children outgrow night terrors by their teenage years.
