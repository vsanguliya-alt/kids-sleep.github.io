Nightmares vs. Night Terrors in 4-Year-Olds: A Parent&#x27;s Guide

At age 4, your child may still experience either nightmares or night terrors—two distinct sleep events that can be confusing and alarming. Understanding the difference is key to knowing how to respond.

Nightmares: Bad Dreams They Remember

A nightmare is a frightening dream that happens during REM (dreaming) sleep, typically in the second half of the night or early morning . Children often wake up fully from a nightmare and remember it in detail .

During a nightmare, your child will:

Wake up scared and upset

Be able to describe the dream afterward

Seek comfort from you and respond to reassurance

Often go back to sleep after being comforted

How to help: Reassure your child with cuddles and calm words . Talk about the dream the next day to help them process their feelings . Avoid scary movies or stories before bedtime, and consider using a dim nightlight if it helps your child feel safer .

Night Terrors: Confusing Sleep Episodes They Won&#x27;t Remember

A night terror is a type of parasomnia—a disruptive sleep behavior—that occurs during deep, non-REM sleep, usually in the first part of the night, often between midnight and 2 a.m. .

Night terrors are most common in children ages 3 through 7 and affect about 1% of children . Unlike nightmares, children usually don&#x27;t remember anything about their night terrors in the morning .

During a night terror, your child will:

Scream, shout, or cry uncontrollably

Sit up in bed with a wide-eyed, terrified stare

Sweat, breathe heavily, and have a racing pulse

Kick and thrash

Be hard or impossible to wake up

Not respond to comfort or soothing

Have no memory of the event the next morning

Common triggers include: serious lack of sleep, stress, fever, schedule changes, and sleep interruptions . Family history of sleep terrors or sleepwalking also increases risk .

What to Do During a Night Terror

Do NOT try to wake your child—this can confuse and frighten them more and may make the episode last longer . Instead:

Stay calm and stay with your child

Ensure their safety (block stairways, lock windows, clear the floor of tripping hazards)

Speak softly and calmly

Gently guide them back to bed if they get up

Wait patiently; episodes typically resolve within minutes

When to Call the Doctor

Occasional night terrors are not usually a cause for concern; mention them at a routine well-child exam . However, contact your pediatrician if night terrors:

Happen often or regularly

Regularly disrupt the family&#x27;s sleep

Lead to safety concerns or injury

Result in daytime symptoms of extreme sleepiness

Continue after age 12 or start after age 5

Are accompanied by snoring or pauses in breathing (may indicate sleep apnea)

Most children outgrow night terrors by their teenage years .
